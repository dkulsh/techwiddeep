*Sequel of [Stubbornness of Beliefs]({{< relref "stubbornness-of-beliefs.md" >}}).*

*This one's from Annie Duke's* Thinking in Bets*. It's the most uncomfortable idea I've read about intelligence: being smart doesn't protect you from bias — it can make your bias harder to catch.*

> The popular wisdom is that the smarter you are, the less susceptible you are to fake news — after all, being "smart" means being good at processing information. Surprisingly, being smart can actually make our biases worse: the smarter you are, the better you are at spinning a story to fit your beliefs.
>
> In 2012, psychologists tested the "bias blind spot" — people who are good at spotting biased reasoning in others but blind to it in themselves. In six of the seven biases tested, more intelligent participants showed *larger* bias blind spots. As Dan Kahan put it: "The better you are with numbers, the better you are at spinning those numbers to conform to and support your beliefs."
>
> This is just the way evolution built us. We're wired to protect our beliefs even when our goal is to truth-seek — and, as with visual illusions, we can't make our minds work differently than they do, no matter how smart we are.
>
> — Annie Duke, *Thinking in Bets*

*The one thing I'd add,* for a room full of smart engineers: the risky person isn't the junior who says "I don't know." It's the senior who can rationalize any decision after the fact. The only defense I've found is to catch yourself mid-argument and ask: *am I reasoning here, or am I rationalizing?*
