We techies often overlook our clothes and dressing sense. This post is a nudge to change that.

Some of what follows may sound prejudiced — partly because it's tangled up with cultural differences.

#### My Journey from clueless to less clueless

Growing up in a small North Indian town, I never realized how much "looks" mattered. I saw fancy-looking folks, but their extra effort seemed like a waste of time — why bother impressing people who didn't matter? ![North Indian style icon](/images/one-techie-to-another-dress-up/north-indian-style-icon.png)

Then I moved to South India, where styles were simpler still. South Indians lead modest lives, and it shows in how they dress. Even as a plainly dressed North Indian, it was a shock — I'd seen dull colours before, but never such disregard for combinations, accessories, and overall appearance. I respected the difference and left it at that.

> A happy side-effect: my underdressed North Indian vibe suddenly made me the *overdressed* one in South India.

![Cultural dressing differences](/images/one-techie-to-another-dress-up/cultural-dressing-differences.jpg)

Later I moved to the US for work, and got the opposite surprise. Westerners are conscious of how they present themselves. Crisp ironed shirts, polished shoes, clean-shaven or neatly trimmed beards — most people looked sharp. Europeans took it a step further.
![European style icon](/images/one-techie-to-another-dress-up/european-style-icon.png)

That stint flipped my perspective. Dressing well isn't only cultural — it's a statement. It isn't always about vanity; often it's about respect, for yourself and for the people around you. ![European style icon](/images/one-techie-to-another-dress-up/european-style-icon-2.png)


#### Why Bother?

A picture's worth a thousand words, so pause on these:

![](images/one-techie-to-another-dress-up/product-packaging-1.jpg) ![](images/one-techie-to-another-dress-up/product-packaging-2.jpg)

![](images/one-techie-to-another-dress-up/product-packaging-3.jpg) ![](images/one-techie-to-another-dress-up/product-packaging-comparison.jpg)

Ask yourself:
- How were the colour combinations?
- Did the packaging make the product more usable?
- Did it make you *want* the product more?
- Would the better-packaged one sell for more?

Good packaging does boost value. If we engineers are the product, our clothes are the packaging, and our employers are the clients paying our salaries. Would your "client" pay more for a sharper-looking you? I think they would.


#### Dress Like a King, Get Treated Like One

Ever met an army officer or a top-notch doctor? I live near an army cantonment and saw this firsthand. A young woman — an officer's daughter — stepped out of a sleek SUV with a sharp-looking jawan. At the dentist she was treated like royalty: skipped the queue, no questions asked. Meanwhile, I waited.

> Dress like a king, and the world treats you like one.

![](images/one-techie-to-another-dress-up/army-officer-treatment.jpg)

We're superficial creatures, admit it. Your secret crush, the head-turner on the street, your Instagram favourites — they're usually well put-together. Dressing well meets people's expectations of "greatness." Dress like a commoner, and you'll be paid like one.


#### What Your Clothes Say About You

Your wardrobe is saying something whether you like it or not. Dress *better* than your job description and you're saying, "I'm more than this role." Dress below it and you're whispering, "I'm not enough."

An engineer who looks sharp in the middle of a P1 outage is saying, "I value myself; I'm in control." A manager with polished shoes amid chaos is saying, "I've got this."

> Your clothes are a non-verbal megaphone. You can't *not* communicate — so choose deliberately.

Leaders read these signals. A VP who sees a well-dressed engineer thinks, "If they nail the small stuff like shoes, they probably nail the big stuff too." Guess who comes to mind at promotion time.


#### Why Do we Techies Resist?

Dressing up feels like a chore. We love coding, not clothes, and time on outfits feels stolen from problem-solving. Fair — but hear me out.

![Engineering work illustration](/images/one-techie-to-another-dress-up/engineering-work-illustration.jpg)

We make time for coffee breaks and meetings (don't get me started — see my rant on [useless meetings]({{< relref "meetings-the-clusterfuck-of-it-industry.md" >}})). Why? They're habits. Dressing well can be a habit too — routine, like brushing your teeth — and the results compound over time.


#### The Social Proof Trap

We look at Mark Zuckerberg's grey tees or Steve Jobs' turtlenecks and think, "They dress down, so why can't I?" The catch: they're already legends. Their success buys them the right to skip the dress code. We're not there yet.

![Zuckerberg Jobs example](/images/one-techie-to-another-dress-up/zuckerberg-jobs-example.jpg)

I worked with a guy in crumpled shirts and shabby trousers. People whispered that he didn't "look like a leader" — but he was already successful, so he didn't care. For those of us still climbing, dressing poorly isn't a luxury we can afford.

> Don't misread the social proof — dress for the success you're chasing, not the success you already have.


#### Avoid the Vanity Trap

Let's keep it real: dressing well isn't about ego, it's about strategy. Overdo it and you're just preening. I don't always dress to the nines — I aim for sharp within reason. Balance is the point.

> Dress well, but don't fall through the vanity trapdoor.

![Vanity trap illustration](/images/one-techie-to-another-dress-up/vanity-trap-illustration.jpg)


That's my take — now suit yourselves!
